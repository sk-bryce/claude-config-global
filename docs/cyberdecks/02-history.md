# History

## Fictional Origins (1984)

The word and the concept both start with William Gibson's 1984 novel *Neuromancer*. Its
protagonist, Henry Dorsett Case, is a washed-up computer hacker ("console cowboy") who uses a
"custom cyberspace deck" to project his consciousness into the Matrix, Gibson's fictional global
data network, described as "a consensual hallucination." Gibson gave almost no hardware
specification for the deck; what mattered narratively was that it was portable, personal, and
routinely modified by its owner to fit their own needs and skill. That single detail, that a
serious user's deck is customized by hand, is arguably the one piece of Gibson's fiction that
turned directly into a real hobby decades later.

The concept was picked up and given more concrete (if still fictional) form by later cyberpunk
media: the tabletop RPGs *Cyberpunk 2020* and later *Cyberpunk RED* (R. Talsorian Games) and
*Shadowrun* (FASA) both built out detailed in-universe deck hardware and rules, and the 2020
video game *Cyberpunk 2077* reintroduced the word to a mainstream audience decades after
Gibson coined it.

## Real-World Precedents Before the Term Existed

Long before anyone was building a "cyberdeck" on purpose, the raw ingredients of the hobby, DIY
personal computing and portable computing, were both already established:

- **The Homebrew Computer Club** (Menlo Park, California, active March 1975 to December 1986),
  founded by Gordon French and Fred Moore, is the clearest ancestor of the cyberdeck-building
  ethos: hobbyists building their own personal computers from raw parts, sharing designs, and
  explicitly rejecting the idea that computing should be something only corporations built and
  sold to you finished. The club's most famous alumni, Steve Wozniak and Steve Jobs, built the
  Apple I there; early Apple I buyers had to source their own case (owners commonly used wooden
  boxes or suitcases), a detail modern deckers will find familiar.
- **Early portable/luggable computers**, such as the Osborne 1 (1981) and later clamshell
  designs like the GRiD Compass (1982), established the idea of a self-contained, battery- or
  mains-powered computer with an integrated screen and keyboard that could be carried and used
  away from a desk. The GRiD Compass in particular, with its rugged magnesium clamshell case, is
  frequently cited by modern builders as a direct visual and conceptual ancestor of the
  cyberdeck form factor.

## From Fiction to Hardware: Early Homebrew Decks (2010s)

For roughly three decades after *Neuromancer*, "cyberdeck" mostly stayed a term for the
fictional prop. What changed it into a real DIY hardware category was the arrival of cheap,
capable single-board computers, above all the Raspberry Pi (first released 2012), which for the
first time gave hobbyists a full Linux computer small and inexpensive enough to embed inside a
scratch-built enclosure without the machine itself being the expensive or hard part of the
project.

Individually documented homebrew builds explicitly invoking the "cyberspace deck" concept began
appearing and circulating online in the mid-to-late 2010s (early examples were widely shared by
outlets like Boing Boing around 2018), and specialty hardware publication Hackaday was already
covering real-world cyberdeck projects in detail by this period, giving the nascent hobby a
consistent publication home and a shared vocabulary.

## Jay Doscher and the "Pelican Case Craze" (2019-2020)

The single most influential figure in crystallizing the modern movement is maker Jay Doscher.
Doscher had been experimenting with rugged, portable, low-cost Raspberry Pi builds for years
beforehand (an early "Raspberry Pi Field Unit" among them), but his 2019 **Raspberry Pi
Recovery Kit**, a ruggedized all-in-one computer built inside a waterproof Pelican-style case,
complete with integrated battery, network switch, and electromagnetic shielding, is widely
credited with
defining the look and philosophy of the modern hobby and kicking off what the community calls
the "Pelican case craze": ruggedized decks built inside surplus waterproof cases designed to
survive drops, water, and rough shipping. Doscher has been explicit that his goal was
practical, not just aesthetic: durable, portable, hackable machines, inspired by but not
slavishly recreating Gibson's fiction. He followed up with further well-known builds, including
an ARM Terminal inspired by *The Matrix* and a Kuiper Deck inspired by *The Expanse* and
*Seveneves*, each pushing the "practical sci-fi field computer" aesthetic in a different
fictional direction.

## Community Formalization: Hackaday Contests and r/cyberDeck (2020-2023)

As more builders published their own decks, the hobby formed real infrastructure around itself:

- The **r/cyberDeck** subreddit became the hobby's central gathering point, growing to roughly
  94,000 members by early 2025, with builders posting several new build logs a day.
- **Hackaday.io ran dedicated Cyberdeck Contests in 2022 and 2023**, drawing well over 100
  serious entries in the first year alone, ranging from laser-cut steel enclosures to custom
  mechanical keyboards with bespoke firmware to reclaimed CRT displays from 1970s portable
  televisions retrofitted with modern compute boards. These contests gave the hobby a recurring
  showcase and pushed build quality and documentation standards up noticeably year over year.

## Recent Growth and Diversification (2020s)

Two things happened at roughly the same time to broaden the hobby well past its original
"rugged field computer" center of gravity:

- **Falling hardware costs and better tooling** (cheap SBCs, accessible 3D printing, small
  battery-management boards) lowered the skill and cost floor for a first build substantially.
- **Short-form video platforms (TikTok, Instagram) became a major discovery and teaching
  channel** starting in the mid-2020s, which both grew the community rapidly and diversified its
  aesthetic vocabulary well beyond the original industrial/military "salvage tech" look. Builders
  working in more decorative, craft-influenced styles, macrame and weaving worked around exposed
  boards, embroidery, found natural materials, pastel and "hyper-feminine" designs, brought a
  different visual language into a hobby that had previously skewed heavily toward stark,
  utilitarian industrial design, alongside continuing minimalist and writer-focused builds. Many
  of these builders describe their motivation less as function and more as a deliberate,
  visible rejection of sealed, black-box consumer electronics and of surveillance-driven "big
  tech" products, treating a hand-built, fully understood machine as a kind of quiet protest as
  much as a tool.

## Where the History Leaves the Hobby Today

The result is a hobby with two threads that coexist without much friction: a functional,
field-tool lineage descended from Doscher's rugged Pelican-case builds and used today for
things like security research and radio work (see [Modern Usage](03-modern-usage.md)), and a
much broader, aesthetics-first craft lineage where the finished object's look and the
experience of building it by hand matter as much as, or more than, what it does. Most real
builds sit somewhere between the two, which is itself very much in keeping with Gibson's
original, deliberately vague description of a deck as whatever its owner made of it.
