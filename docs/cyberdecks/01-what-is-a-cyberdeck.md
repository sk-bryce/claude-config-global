# What Is a Cyberdeck

## Working Definition

A cyberdeck is a custom-built, portable computer that its maker designs and assembles largely
from scratch, usually around a single-board computer (SBC) or a repurposed compute module,
paired with a self-contained display, keyboard, and power system, and housed in a hand-built
enclosure rather than a mass-produced laptop shell. It is typically built for a specific,
individualized purpose (security research, radio work, offline note-taking, and so on) and
often carries a deliberately rugged, exposed, "hand-assembled" look often described as
cyberpunk or retro-futurist.

The two things that separate a cyberdeck from an ordinary DIY computer project are:

- **Portability as a requirement, not an afterthought.** It has its own battery, its own
  screen, and its own input device, so it can be picked up and used away from a desk.
- **The enclosure and industrial design are part of the build, not an off-the-shelf case.**
  A Raspberry Pi in a commercial project box is a Raspberry Pi project; the same board wired
  into a hand-fabricated chassis with an exposed keyboard and a deliberate visual language is
  a cyberdeck. The build itself, and how visibly "handmade" it is, is part of the point.

People who design and build cyberdecks are often called "deckers," borrowing the term for
console cowboys/hackers in cyberpunk fiction. A cyberdeck built specifically around long-form
writing, usually a distraction-free keyboard-and-small-screen device, is sometimes called a
"writerdeck," a recognized sub-category within the hobby.

## Etymology

"Cyberdeck" is built out of vocabulary William Gibson established in his 1984 novel
*Neuromancer*, the foundational text of the cyberpunk genre. Gibson writes of "cyberspace" and
of the "deck" a hacker jacks in with; the fused compound "cyberdeck" came into general use
later, through the tabletop cyberpunk RPGs and fan communities that built on him. In the novel
and its sequels, a deck (also called a cyberspace deck) is a portable terminal that a "console
cowboy" uses to jack into the Matrix, Gibson's global data network, often via electrodes wired
directly to the user's nervous system. Gibson deliberately left the hardware details vague:
what he established was the idea, not the design, that these devices are portable, personal,
and frequently modified by their owners to suit their own needs and style. That last detail,
that serious users customize their own decks, turned out to be the seed of the entire
real-world hobby decades later.

The term and concept were picked up and elaborated on across cyberpunk media that followed,
most notably the tabletop RPGs *Cyberpunk* (and its *Cyberpunk 2020*/*Cyberpunk RED* editions)
and *Shadowrun*, both of which gave cyberdecks more concrete stat blocks and visual designs,
and later the 2020 video game *Cyberpunk 2077*, which reintroduced the term to a much wider
audience. The word moved from a purely fictional device to a real, buildable category of
hardware once cheap single-board computers made "just build the thing from the book" practical
for hobbyists.

## Core Characteristics

Real-world cyberdecks vary enormously, but builds that the community recognizes as cyberdecks
tend to share some combination of the following:

- **Self-contained power.** A battery (commonly a rechargeable lithium pack) and its own charge
  and protection circuitry, so the device does not depend on being plugged in to a desk.
- **Integrated display.** Usually a small LCD panel repurposed from another product, though
  e-ink and even reclaimed CRT displays show up for specific aesthetics or use cases.
- **Exposed or deliberately visible construction.** Unlike a laptop, which hides its
  engineering behind an injection-molded shell, a cyberdeck often shows its wiring, its
  fasteners, or its structural members as part of the design language, sometimes literally
  (a see-through or open-frame chassis) and sometimes just through a utilitarian, unpolished
  aesthetic.
- **A keyboard chosen and mounted by the builder**, rather than a laptop's integrated keyboard.
  Mechanical keyboards, keyboard kits, and salvaged keyboards from other devices are all common.
- **General-purpose compute**, almost always running a full desktop or server-class operating
  system (Linux distributions dominate) rather than a fixed-function embedded firmware, so the
  finished device is a real, usable computer and not just a prop.
- **A purpose.** Even the most aesthetics-driven builds tend to be built around at least one
  real capability: running a specific set of tools, operating specific radios, hosting a
  specific offline dataset, and so on. See [Modern Usage](03-modern-usage.md) for the range of
  purposes people build around today.

## How It Differs From Adjacent Categories

- **Vs. a laptop:** a laptop is a finished consumer product; a cyberdeck is assembled by its
  owner from discrete parts, and its form factor, capability set, and appearance are all
  choices the builder made rather than choices a manufacturer made for them.
- **Vs. a ruggedized field computer (e.g., a Panasonic Toughbook):** ruggedized computers are
  commercial products engineered by a manufacturer to a durability spec; a cyberdeck's
  durability, if any, is whatever the builder designed into their own enclosure. Some cyberdecks
  are genuinely rugged; many are not, and prioritize looks or a specific capability instead.
- **Vs. a bare SBC project board (e.g., a Raspberry Pi in a 3D-printed box):** the distinction is
  fuzzy and the community does not police it strictly, but the general expectation is that a
  cyberdeck is a complete, self-contained, portable computer you can pick up and use, with its
  own screen, input, and power, not a board that still needs a monitor and keyboard plugged in
  to be useful.

## Two Dimensions: Function and Aesthetic

Builds in this space tend to sit somewhere on two largely independent axes, and it helps to
keep them separate when thinking about your own build (see the [Build Guide](04-build-guide.md)):

- **Functional intent:** what the machine is actually for, from a serious field tool (radio
  direction-finding, penetration-testing rigs) to a purely recreational or artistic object with
  little practical function beyond being a satisfying object to hold and look at.
- **Aesthetic intent:** how the machine is meant to look and feel, from stark
  industrial/military "salvage tech," to the classic 1980s-cyberpunk "console cowboy" look, to
  the softer, decorative, "hyper-feminine" and craft-influenced styles (macrame, embroidery,
  found natural materials) that have become prominent in the community more recently, to a
  minimalist, almost furniture-grade design language with no genre signaling at all.

Neither axis determines the other: a heavily decorated, aesthetics-forward deck can still run
serious offline tooling, and a stark, all-business enclosure can be built purely as a display
piece. Deciding where your own project sits on both axes early is one of the most useful
planning steps before you start picking parts.
